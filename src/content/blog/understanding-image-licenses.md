"Free" is not one thing. A free image can still come with conditions, and ignoring them is the most common way creators get into trouble — a takedown notice, a bill, or worse if it's for a client. Here's what actually matters, in plain language.

## The licenses you'll meet most

- **Public Domain / CC0** — no rights reserved. Use freely, for anything, no credit needed. The safest option there is.
- **CC BY** — free to use, including commercially, as long as you **credit the creator**.
- **CC BY-SA** — same as CC BY, but any derivative you make must be shared under the same license. Fine for a blog post, awkward for a closed commercial product.
- **CC BY-NC** — the "NC" means **non-commercial only**. Great for a school project, off-limits for anything that makes money.
- **Provider licenses** (Unsplash, Pexels, Pixabay) — permissive and commercial-friendly, each with their own small print (usually: don't resell the photo as-is, and don't imply the person in it endorses you).

## When do you have to give credit?

Whenever the license says "BY". If attribution is required, include three things: the creator's name, the source, and the license — ideally as links. A clean format looks like:

> Photo by *Creator Name* on *Source*, licensed under *CC BY 4.0*.

When credit isn't required, adding it anyway is still a kind gesture that supports the people making this work freely available.

## The traps that catch people out

- **"Non-commercial" is stricter than it sounds.** A monetised blog, a client project, or an ad all count as commercial.
- **Editorial-only images** can show logos, landmarks or recognisable faces that you *can't* use to sell a product, even if the photo is free.
- **People and property need releases** for commercial use. A free license covers the photo, not necessarily the right to use someone's face in an advert.
- **Licenses can change**, but the version you downloaded under still applies — which is why keeping a record matters.

## Staying safe in commercial work

- Read the license **before** you download, not after.
- Keep a simple record of where each asset came from and under what license.
- When in doubt, prefer **CC0** or a **provider license** for commercial projects.
- For anything high-stakes (a paid ad, a product package), lean toward images cleared for commercial use with no attribution requirement.

## FAQ

**Does "free to use" mean I can do anything with it?**
No. It means free of cost, but the license still sets the rules — especially around credit and commercial use.

**Do I need a lawyer to use free images?**
For everyday content, no — just follow the license. For large campaigns or anything involving recognisable people, a quick legal check is worth it.
